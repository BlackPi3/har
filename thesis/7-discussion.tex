\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter interprets the experimental findings presented in \Cref{chap:results}, focusing on what the results suggest about the conditions under which pose-to-IMU simulation\cite{kwon2020imutube} benefits HAR performance.

\section{Findings}

\paragraph{Dataset Size.}
Perhaps the most consistent pattern across all experiments is the divergence between UTD-MHAD\cite{chen2015utd} and MM-Fit\cite{stromback2020mm}: nearly every training variant improves performance on UTD-MHAD while degrading it on MM-Fit, where the baseline already achieves the highest F1. This pattern could reflects the role of dataset size in determining whether simulated data provides meaningful augmentation. UTD-MHAD, with only 4 training subjects, likely lacks sufficient real-data diversity for the feature extractor to learn robust representations on its own. In this data-scarce regime, the additional variation introduced by simulated signals may act as a regularizer that improves generalization to unseen subjects. MM-Fit, with substantially more training data, probably already provides enough coverage for the baseline architecture to perform well without supplementary signals. In that case, the simulated path may introduce systematic distributional differences that the feature extractor must accommodate, potentially at the cost of performance on real test data. Notably, the HPO search space for MM-Fit included regularization parameters (dropout rates, weight decay) and capacity parameters (kernel sizes, hidden layer dimensions), giving the optimizer freedom to adjust model complexity in either direction. Despite this, no configuration of the training variants managed to match the baseline on MM-Fit, suggesting that the degradation is probably not a simple case of overfitting that could be resolved through regularization or capacity tuning.

\paragraph{Domain Gap.}
The contrasting outcomes of the two adversarial approaches offer some insight into where the sim-to-real domain gap is most pronounced. Feature-level adversarial training (Scenario 4.1)\cite{ganin2016domain} successfully achieves domain confusion, as the discriminator fluctuates near chance accuracy, yet classification performance degrades on both datasets. Signal-level adversarial training (Scenario 4.2)\cite{arjovsky2017wasserstein,gulrajani2017improved}, by contrast, improves classification on UTD-MHAD while also achieving discriminator confusion. One interpretation is that the residual domain gap is more pronounced in the raw signal space than in the learned feature space. The shared feature extractor, combined with the explicit similarity loss, may already produce reasonably domain-invariant representations, making additional adversarial pressure at the feature level redundant or even counterproductive, as the adversarial objective could push the extractor to discard information that, while domain-specific, is also task-relevant. Signal-level adversarial training targets the regressor directly, encouraging it to produce accelerometer signals whose distribution more closely matches real data before they enter the feature extraction pipeline. This complements the MSE loss in a different way: MSE penalizes point-wise deviation while the adversarial loss encourages distributional realism. This combination helps on UTD-MHAD but not MM-Fit probably ties back to dataset size. The regressor may already produce signals that are sufficient, and adversarial refinement adds training complexity without proportional benefit. It is worth noting that the adversarial loss does not merely fail to help on MM-Fit. It degrades performance. This is probably because adversarial training is not a passive addition: the discriminator gradients actively modify either the feature extractor or the regressor to satisfy the domain-invariance objective. When the baseline is already near-optimal, as appears to be the case on MM-Fit, these competing gradients may push the model away from a configuration that the classification objective alone would have found. On UTD-MHAD, where the baseline has more room to improve, this search may land in a better region of the loss landscape. The exact mechanism behind the degradation on MM-Fit remains unclear, but the pattern is consistent with the broader observation that auxiliary training objectives carry a risk of interference when the primary task is already well-served by the available data.


\paragraph{Regression Loss as a Necessary Anchor.}
The significant failure observed when removing the regression loss ($\gamma = 0$) reveals something about the learning dynamics in this framework. Without direct signal-level supervision, the regressor receives gradients only through the classification loss, backpropagated through the feature extractor. This gradient path is indirect: the classification objective provides no explicit information about what the simulated signal should look like, only about whether the downstream features lead to correct predictions. In principle, the regressor could learn to produce arbitrary signals. In practice, it appears that this freedom does not lead to a correct space, wichi leads to producing outputs with magnitudes orders of magnitude beyond the real data range. The MSE loss likely serves as a necessary anchor that constrains the regressor to remain in a physically plausible output space. Without this grounding, the feature extractor is probably forced to accommodate increasingly unrealistic inputs, which may corrupt its learned representations for real data as well. The feature similarity loss provides a related but weaker form of supervision at the representation level. Its removal causes more modest degradation, suggesting it probably functions as additional regularization rather than a fundamental training requirement. The larger effect on MM-Fit compared to UTD-MHAD may indicate that explicit feature alignment becomes more important when the model has enough capacity and data to overfit to domain-specific patterns. More broadly, the $\gamma = 0$ result illustrates a structural property of the shared-weight architecture: the feature extractor cannot simply learn to ignore the simulated path when it produces unhelpful inputs. Because both real and simulated data pass through the same feature extractor, poor simulated signals actively shape the learned representations rather than being passively discarded. This property likely extends beyond the extreme case of chaotic regressor outputs, even when simulated signals are structurally reasonable but suboptimal for classification, the shared feature extractor must still accommodate them, potentially at the expense of its representations for real data. This may partly explain the consistent degradation observed on MM-Fit across several scenarios, though the precise mechanisms through which each variant interferes with an already well-performing baseline remain an open question.

\paragraph{Auxiliary Data and Domain Compatibility.}
The auxiliary NTU experiments suggest that the value of additional pose data depends more on domain compatibility than on quantity. NTU RGB+D\cite{shahroudy2016ntu} provides substantially more pose diversity than either primary dataset, with 60 action classes and 40 subjects. Yet this diversity appears to help only when the auxiliary activities share kinematic characteristics with the target task. UTD-MHAD, with roughly 33\% activity overlap with NTU, shows modest improvement, while MM-Fit, with only about 9\% overlap, degrades considerably. The per-class analysis complicates the picture further: improvements do not simply track the presence of direct NTU equivalents. Some UTD-MHAD activities without NTU matches improve, while some with semantic matches degrade. This pattern suggests the auxiliary data probably provides general motion diversity rather than activity-specific transfer, the regressor, exposed to a broader range of pose-to-motion mappings, may develop more flexible internal representations that generalize differently across activity classes. The consistent selection of a low auxiliary loss weight ($\lambda_{\text{aux}} = 0.25$) on both datasets suggests that the optimization process treats auxiliary data as supplementary rather than primary. When domain overlap is poor, even this modest weighting may allow gradients from incompatible activities to interfere with primary-task learning.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Baseline Performance.}
Our baseline results on both UTD-MHAD\cite{chen2015utd} and MM-Fit\cite{stromback2020mm} fall slightly below those reported in \cite{zolfaghari2024sensor}, though not by a large margin. Since both codebases were developed by the same author, this discrepancy is unlikely to stem from differences in implementation quality. The earlier codebase produces similar results when re-evaluated, suggesting the gap may arise from differences in preprocessing, data splitting, or training configuration that have not yet been isolated. This remains an unresolved issue and should be taken into account when comparing absolute performance figures across the two works.

\paragraph{Generalizability.}
The experiments in this work are limited to two primary datasets that differ substantially in size, activity type, and sensor characteristics. While this diversity provides some confidence that the observed patterns are not artifacts of a single dataset, two datasets are not sufficient to draw broadly generalizable conclusions. Evaluating the proposed training scenarios on additional HAR datasets would help clarify which findings are robust and which are dataset-specific.

\paragraph{Adversarial Training Variants.}
The two adversarial scenarios each use a fixed combination of discriminator level and training strategy: feature-level with GRL\cite{ganin2016domain} (Scenario 4.1) and signal-level with WGAN-GP\cite{arjovsky2017wasserstein,gulrajani2017improved} (Scenario 4.2). This design leaves open the question of whether the observed differences are driven by where the discriminator operates or by how it is trained. Testing a WGAN-GP variant at the feature level could reveal whether the underperformance of Scenario 4.1 stems from the BCE/GRL training dynamics rather than from feature-level discrimination itself. Conversely, applying GRL to the signal-level discriminator would help isolate whether the signal-level improvements in Scenario 4.2 depend on the Wasserstein objective or simply on targeting the regressor.

\paragraph{Auxiliary Data Compatibility.}
The auxiliary data experiments used NTU RGB+D\cite{shahroudy2016ntu}, whose clip structure, isolated activity segments without surrounding non-activity periods, is similar to UTD-MHAD and not to MM-Fit. This structural similarity may partly affect the domain compatibility analysis: it is difficult to separate the effect of activity class overlap from the effect of data format compatibility. Repeating Scenario 3 with a secondary dataset that more closely matches MM-Fit in both storage format and activity domain would help disentangle these factors. Additionally, combining auxiliary pose data with adversarial training (i.e., merging Scenarios 3 and 4) remains untested and could reveal whether the two approaches provide complementary benefits or compound the interference observed on larger datasets.
