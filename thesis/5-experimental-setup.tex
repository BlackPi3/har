\chapter{Experimental Setup}
\label{chap:experimental-setup}

This chapter describes the experimental framework used to evaluate our proposed method. We detail the datasets employed, preprocessing procedures, evaluation protocols, and the experimental scenarios used to systematically analyze the contribution of each component.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate our method on two primary datasets that provide synchronized skeleton pose and inertial measurement unit (IMU) data: UTD-MHAD and MM-Fit. Additionally, we use NTU RGB+D as a secondary pose-only dataset for auxiliary training experiments.

\paragraph{UTD-MHAD.}
The University of Texas at Dallas Multimodal Human Action Dataset (UTD-MHAD) \cite{chen2015utd} is a benchmark dataset for multimodal action recognition. It captures human activities using both a Microsoft Kinect sensor and a wearable inertial sensor. The dataset has 8 subjects, 21 activity classes including gestures, sports movements, and daily activities. It has 671 actions clips with approximately 4 repetitions per subject and per action. It has both 3D skeleton pose sequence sampled at 30 Hz and 3D accelerometer sampled at 50 Hz. The Kinect sensor captures 3D joint positions at 30 fps. We upsample them to 50 Hz to match the inertial sensor. The wearable sensor is placed on the subject's right wrist for upper-body actions and right thigh for lower-body actions. We use a subject-disjoint split to evaluate generalization to unseen individuals. For training we use subjects 1--4, for validation subjects 5--6 and for test subjects 7--8. This split ensures that models are evaluated on subjects never seen during training or hyperparameter tuning.

\paragraph{MM-Fit.}
The MM-Fit dataset \cite{stromback2020mm} captures fitness activities in a gym setting, providing synchronized video and wearable sensor data. The dataset has 21 subjects performing structured workout sessions across 11 activity classes: 10 exercises (squats, lunges, bicep curls, situps, pushups, tricep extensions, dumbbell rows, jumping jacks, dumbbell shoulder press, lateral shoulder raises) plus a ``no activity'' class for rest periods. Each session comprises 3 sets of 10 exercises with 10 repetitions each. It provides 3D skeleton pose sequences (17 joints) extracted from video at 30 fps and 3-axis accelerometer data from a left wrist smartwatch sampled at 100 Hz. Unlike UTD-MHAD which contains discrete action clips, MM-Fit provides continuous recordings where activities are annotated with start and end timestamps, requiring careful windowing to handle activity boundaries. For training we use 12 subjects (w00--w08, w16--w18), for validation 4 subjects (w14, w15, w19, w20), and for test 4 subjects (w09--w11, w13). One subject (w12) is excluded due to data quality issues. The splits follow the protocol established in the original MM-Fit paper.

\paragraph{NTU RGB+D.}
The NTU RGB+D dataset \cite{shahroudy2016ntu} is a large-scale action recognition benchmark captured with Microsoft Kinect v2 sensors. We use it as a secondary dataset for auxiliary pose training experiments. The dataset has 40 subjects performing 60 action classes spanning daily activities, mutual interactions, and health-related actions, with over 56,000 video clips in total. It provides 3D skeleton sequences only (25 joints from Kinect v2 body tracking) with no IMU data, serving exclusively as a source of additional pose sequences for training the regressor (\Cref{sec:scenario-auxiliary}). For training we use subjects 1--30, for validation subjects 31--35, and for test subjects 36--40. To match the sampling rates of primary datasets, we resample NTU to 50 Hz for use with UTD-MHAD and to 100 Hz for use with MM-Fit.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Raw sensor data requires several preprocessing steps before training. This section describes the normalization, alignment, and segmentation procedures applied to each modality.

\paragraph{Skeleton Normalization.}
Skeleton pose data exhibits significant variation across subjects due to differences in body proportions and global positioning. We apply a two-stage normalization procedure to ensure scale and translation invariance. First, all skeleton sequences are centered relative to the hip position in the first frame: $\mathbf{p}'_{t,j} = \mathbf{p}_{t,j} - \mathbf{p}_{1,\text{hip}}$, where $\mathbf{p}_{t,j} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the position of joint $j$ at timestep $t$. Using the first-frame hip as reference (rather than per-frame centering) preserves the global motion trajectory. Second, to account for varying body sizes, we scale all coordinates by the median torso length $s = \text{median}_{t} \|\mathbf{p}_{t,\text{head}} - \mathbf{p}_{t,\text{hip}}\|_2$, computing $\mathbf{p}''_{t,j} = \mathbf{p}'_{t,j}/s$. Using the median provides robustness to outlier frames, and a minimum threshold of $10^{-4}$ prevents division by near-zero values.

\paragraph{Temporal Alignment.}
When skeleton and accelerometer data have different temporal lengths (due to frame drops or sampling rate mismatches), we align them through linear interpolation. We define normalized time axes $t_{\text{skel}} = \text{linspace}(0, 1, N_{\text{skel}})$ and $t_{\text{acc}} = \text{linspace}(0, 1, N_{\text{acc}})$, then interpolate skeleton positions to match accelerometer timestamps. This produces synchronized pose and accelerometer sequences of equal length.

\paragraph{Accelerometer Standardization.}
Accelerometer signals are standardized to zero mean and unit variance: $\mathbf{a}_{\text{std}} = (\mathbf{a} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}})/(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}} + \epsilon)$, where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are the per-axis mean and standard deviation computed over training samples only, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. The same training statistics are applied to validation and test sets to prevent data leakage.

\paragraph{Sliding Window Segmentation.}
Continuous sensor streams are segmented into fixed-length windows for batch processing. For UTD-MHAD we use a window length of 100 samples (2 seconds at 50 Hz) with stride of 5 samples (0.1 seconds). For MM-Fit we treat the window size as a hyperparameter whose best performing value is found during hyper-parameters optimization (HPO). The reason behind this decision is how MM-Fit data set is stored which allows for using many options as window size. We, however, set the stride to 20 samples (0.2 seconds). The shorter stride relative to window length creates overlapping windows, effectively augmenting the training data. For datasets with per-frame labels (MM-Fit), each window is assigned the label of the activity occurring at its temporal midpoint; windows spanning activity boundaries are included if at least 50\% of frames belong to the assigned activity. Action clips shorter than the window length are padded by replicating edge values, which preserves temporal characteristics better than zero-padding.

\paragraph{Joint Selection.}
In this thesis we don't use all of the joints in pose sequence. For pose-to-IMU regression, we select the three arm joints corresponding to the accelerometer placement location: joints 8, 9, 10 (right shoulder, elbow, wrist) for UTD-MHAD matching the right-wrist sensor, and joints 16, 17, 18 (left shoulder, elbow, wrist) for MM-Fit matching the left-wrist smartwatch. This reduces the input dimensionality from the full skeleton (17--25 joints) to the kinematically relevant arm chain.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation-protocol}

\paragraph{Metric.}
Since our main goal is improving HAR classification, we report \textbf{Macro F1 Score} as our primary metric. It is the unweighted mean of per-class F1 scores, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{F1}_{\text{macro}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{2 \cdot P_k \cdot R_k}{P_k + R_k}
\end{equation}
where $P_k$ and $R_k$ are precision and recall for class $k$. Macro F1 treats all classes equally regardless of sample count, making it appropriate for imbalanced datasets such as MM-Fit where ``no activity'' periods are overrepresented.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter Selection.}
For each scenario, we perform three sequential passes of hyperparameter optimization as described in \Cref{chap:implementation}. All HPO runs are ranked by validation F1. After each pass, we select the top 10 configurations and re-run each with 5 different seeds, computing mean and standard deviation to identify the truly best-performing parameter set rather than one that succeeded due to a lucky initialization. The winning configuration from each pass is fixed before proceeding to the next. During HPO, we use 40\% of full training epochs to speed up the search.

\paragraph{Final Evaluation.}
After completing all three HPO passes, we take the best configuration and run final evaluation 10 times with different seeds using the full training epoch budget. We report test set performance as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over these 10 runs. Test set evaluation is performed only after HPO is complete to prevent implicit overfitting through repeated evaluation.
